Homework 4 · Stochastic processes

Stochastic penetration processes

m attackers, n systems, and a score added at each step. Simulating Sₙ, Sₙ/n and Sₙ/√n shows the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem at work.

01

Homework

Consider n systems and m attackers. Model the penetration process as a stochastic process with i.i.d. increments X₁, X₂, …, the penetration score at each step, under two models:

  • Bernoulli: Xᵢ ∈ {0, 1} with P(Xᵢ = 1) = p.
  • Rademacher: Xᵢ ∈ {−1, +1} with P(Xᵢ = +1) = P(Xᵢ = −1) = ½, the symmetric, zero-mean case.

For increasing n and suitable m, simulate sample trajectories and study the distributions of the total score Sₙ = Σ Xᵢ, the average score Sₙ/n and the normalized total Sₙ/√n. Investigate the convergence in probability of Sₙ/n (LLN), the variance under each normalization, and the convergence in distribution of Sₙ/√n to N(0, 1) in the Rademacher case (CLT). Interpret the trajectories in terms of n systems and m attackers, and discuss how system size and number of attackers affect the aggregate penetration score.

02

The model and what theory predicts

Each attacker j = 1 … m attacks the systems one after another. Step i is the attempt on system i, and its outcome is the increment Xᵢ⁽ʲ⁾. The partial sums S₁, S₂, …, Sₙ form the attacker's trajectory, a random walk. In the Bernoulli model Sₙ counts successful penetrations. In the Rademacher model it is a net score: +1 for a breach, −1 for being detected or blocked.

Bernoulli(p)Rademacher
Mean μ = E[Xᵢ]p0
Variance σ² = Var(Xᵢ)p(1 − p)1
Total Sₙmean np, variance np(1−p) (Binomial)mean 0, variance n
Average Sₙ/nmean p, variance p(1−p)/n → 0mean 0, variance 1/n → 0
Normalized Sₙ/√nmean p√n → ∞, variance p(1−p)mean 0, variance 1 → N(0, 1)

These all follow from the fact that the variance of a sum of independent terms is the sum of their variances: Var(Sₙ) = nσ², so Var(Sₙ/n) = σ²/n and Var(Sₙ/√n) = σ². Dividing by n is too strong, since the fluctuations vanish and only the mean survives (LLN). Not dividing is too weak, since the fluctuations grow like √n. Dividing by √n is exactly right: the fluctuations stay of order one and their shape converges to a normal distribution (CLT).

LLN (weak): P( |Sₙ/n − μ| > ε ) → 0 for every ε > 0 (Chebyshev: ≤ σ² / (n ε²)) CLT: (Sₙ − nμ) / (σ√n) → N(0, 1) in distribution (Rademacher: Sₙ/√n → N(0, 1))

05

Law of Large Numbers: convergence in probability of Sₙ/n

For each n we estimate P(|Sₙ/n − μ| > ε) as the fraction of the m attackers whose average lies more than ε from μ, and compare it with the Chebyshev bound σ²/(nε²). For very small n the curve can briefly rise, because Sₙ/n only takes a few discrete values. In the Rademacher case with n = 10, for instance, |Sₙ/n| ≤ 0.05 requires exactly Sₙ = 0. Once n is large, the probability drops to zero, as the LLN predicts.

08

Interpreting n systems and m attackers

The number of systems n is the length of each trajectory. The number of attackers m is the number of independent trajectories, i.e. how many samples we have of each distribution. They play different roles:

  • More systems (larger n). Each attacker's total score drifts by nμ and spreads by σ√n, so differences between attackers in absolute terms grow. Their success rate Sₙ/n becomes a precise estimate of μ = p. In the Rademacher case the net score has no drift: typical attackers end at about ±√n, and the walk keeps crossing zero.
  • More attackers (larger m). m does not change any single trajectory, but it gives a sharper picture of the distribution: the sample mean of the m scores has standard error σ√n/√m. It also makes extremes more likely. The score of the best of m attackers grows like √(2 ln m) even when nobody has any skill (Rademacher, μ = 0). A large organisation facing many attackers will therefore see some very "successful" ones by chance alone.
  • Aggregate score over all attackers, Σⱼ Sₙ⁽ʲ⁾, is a sum of m·n independent increments. Its mean is m·n·μ and its standard deviation σ√(mn), so the total exposure grows linearly in both n and m while its relative uncertainty shrinks like 1/√(mn).
09

Conclusions

  • Sₙ has variance nσ², so its distribution spreads out without bound. In the Bernoulli model it also drifts upward at rate p. Raw totals are hard to compare across different system sizes.
  • Sₙ/n converges in probability to μ (LLN). Its variance σ²/n goes to zero, and the simulated probability of a deviation larger than ε falls to zero, staying below the Chebyshev bound. With many systems, an attacker's observed success rate reveals their true success probability.
  • Sₙ/√n keeps variance σ² for every n. In the Rademacher case its distribution converges to N(0, 1) (CLT), and the KS distance shrinks as n grows. In the Bernoulli case Sₙ/√n drifts to infinity because μ ≠ 0, so the centred version (Sₙ − np)/√(np(1−p)) is the one that converges to N(0, 1).
  • The √n scaling is what statistical inference relies on: it gives the size of random fluctuations, so we can tell a genuinely skilled attacker (a drift of order n) from a lucky one (a fluctuation of order √n), with the caveat that among many attackers the luckiest one drifts upward, roughly like √(2 ln m) standard units, by chance alone.
